Every lunar landing is a bet on two lines of sight. One to the Sun, for power. One to Earth, for the radio. At the south pole both skim the horizon, and a few days or a few hundred metres can break either.

Why landing near the pole is a timing problem

  1. The Moon barely tilts

    Earth’s axis leans 23.4°, which gives us seasons. The Moon’s leans only 1.54° to the plane of its orbit around the Sun. Stand at the south pole and the Sun never rises or sets in the usual way. It circles the horizon once every 29.5 days, never more than about 1.6° up.

    Axial tiltEarthMoonFig. 01

  2. A few hundred metres of height decide everything

    With the Sun that low, a crater rim casts a shadow kilometres long. The floors of some polar craters have not seen sunlight in billions of years, and that is where water ice survives. A ridge a few hundred metres higher can stay in sunlight for most of the year. A lander needs that sunlight for power and warmth.

    Grazing lightCrater rimCrater floorFig. 02

  3. Earth hangs still, then wobbles

    The Moon keeps one face toward us, so Earth barely moves in the lunar sky. Libration rocks it about 7° up and down and side to side each month. On the equator that hardly matters. At the pole, where Earth sits on the horizon, the wobble decides whether a lander can talk to Earth directly or must wait for a relay.

    LibrationAbove horizonBelow horizonFig. 03

  4. So a two-week slip can change the mission

    Hardware runs late and launch dates move. Each bar below is a 14-day surface mission at the Shackleton–de Gerlache ridge, starting on a different day of 2027, and its height is how much of that mission is in sunlight. Move the start by a fortnight and a well-lit mission can become a dark one.

    Data & method

    Every number in the navigator comes from NASA and JPL data: JPL’s DE421 planetary ephemeris for where the Sun and Earth are, and the Lunar Orbiter Laser Altimeter (LOLA) for the shape of the ground. The heavy work is done once by a Python pipeline; your browser then computes each scenario from those files. Nothing is mocked.

    Where the Sun and Earth are

    Positions come from JPL’s DE421 ephemeris through the Skyfield library: the apparent Sun and Earth as seen from the Moon, with light travel time and aberration, rotated into the Moon’s own body-fixed frame using NAIF’s lunar orientation data. That frame includes the Moon’s real wobble (libration) and is the same frame LOLA maps use, so the sky and the terrain line up.

    The pipeline stores one record per hour for 2026–2030 (1 MB). For a site, the browser turns those vectors into elevation and compass bearing, correcting Earth’s direction for the site’s offset from the Moon’s centre. The result matches JPL Horizons to a few arcseconds (table below). Outside 2026–2030 the site falls back to Meeus’ almanac formulas, accurate to about 0.04°.

    Where the horizon is

    The Moon has no atmosphere to bend light, so a body is visible only when it is geometrically above the skyline. On a smooth sphere, a 2-metre lander mast sees the horizon 0.087° below level. A site on a ridge 600 m above its surroundings sees it about 1.5° below level, which is enough to keep the low polar Sun in view.

    Each site’s real skyline is computed from LOLA elevation maps: 720 rays, one every half degree of compass bearing, each sampled out to 200 km. Along every ray the terrain height becomes an angle seen from a 2-metre mast, with the Moon’s curvature included; the highest angle is the horizon. Near the pole the maps resolve 80 m, to 60°S 240 m, elsewhere about 1.9 km.

    Artemis III regions are areas, not points, so for those the pipeline picks the highest LOLA point within a few kilometres of the region’s centre, where a lander would most likely go. The navigator shows that point.

    What the metrics mean

    Sunlit
    Share of the span when more than half of the Sun’s disc is above the skyline.
    Earth in view
    Share of the span when more than half of Earth’s disc is above the skyline, so a direct radio link is possible.
    Both
    Power and a direct link at the same time: the hours a lander can work and report.
    Longest dark
    The longest unbroken period without sunlight inside the span. Batteries and heaters must survive it.
    Highest Sun
    Peak solar elevation while visible. Low angles favour vertical solar arrays.

    Known limits

    • Horizons are precomputed for the curated sites. Custom coordinates use a smooth-sphere horizon, and the navigator says so.
    • Away from the poles the global LOLA map is about 1.9 km per pixel, so boulders and small craters right next to a lander are not resolved.
    • Artemis III and VIPER regions use an automatically picked high point, not an official landing point.
    • Sunlight is treated as on or off at half the disc; real power also depends on panel orientation, dust and temperature.

    NASA and partner data this builds on

    • JPL Horizons System: precise ephemerides for cross-checking Sun and Earth positions from any lunar site.
    • LOLA gridded elevation models (PDS Geosciences Node): LDEM 80 m and 240 m south-polar maps and the 16 px/deg global map, used for every terrain horizon.
    • JPL DE421 and NAIF lunar orientation kernels: where the Sun and Earth are, in the Moon’s own frame.
    • NASA SVS CGI Moon Kit: LROC colour mosaic and LOLA elevation textures for the 3D Moon.
